Arabic I — Week 01

Arabic sounds and script orientation · Foundations

Learning goal: recognize the direction of Arabic writing, identify five introductory letters, and begin listening and writing with care.

1. Meet the writing system

Arabic is written primarily right to left. Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) provides the shared literacy foundation for this course. Spoken Arabic varies across regions; real conversations may use local varieties. Neither one region nor one accent represents all Arabic speakers.

The Arabic alphabet has 28 basic letters. Several letters change shape depending on their position in a word; we will study connected forms systematically in Weeks 2–3. Short vowel marks are introduced in Week 4. Today, focus on the isolated forms shown below.

Reading reminder: English approximations are not exact Arabic pronunciations. A teacher or a trustworthy native-speaker recording should model sounds; do not assume a device-generated voice is accurate.

2. Five starter letters

ا
Alif · ألف

Usually supports long /ā/ or a hamza-bearing vowel in common spelling. Do not treat isolated ا as a complete pronunciation rule.

ب
Baaʾ · باء

Consonant /b/. One dot below in the isolated form.

ت
Taaʾ · تاء

Consonant /t/. Two dots above.

ث
Thaaʾ · ثاء

Consonant /θ/ in standard pronunciation, similar to “th” in “think.” Three dots above.

ن
Nuun · نون

Consonant /n/. One dot above in the isolated form.

3. Four-skill lesson sequence

  1. Listening: ask a qualified speaker or teacher to model ب /b/, ت /t/, ث /θ/, and ن /n/. Listen twice and identify the contrasting sounds. If no recording or speaker is available, complete the visual work now and mark listening as awaiting teacher demonstration.
  2. Speaking: say each letter's name, then attempt each consonant sound separately. Listen for the distinction between /t/ and /θ/.
  3. Reading: scan the five letter cards from right to left. Point to the correct isolated glyph as a partner says its name.
  4. Writing: copy the five isolated letters on lined paper, paying attention to dot placement. Treat these as copy models, not a substitute for verified stroke-order instruction.
ا　ب　ت　ث　ن

Teacher checkpoint: distinguish the letter shape from the consonant sound, identify handwriting support needs, and use the learner's preferred language when explaining misconceptions.

4. Guided practice and reflection

  • Write ب and ت three times; explain which direction the dots move.
  • Sort these by dot count and placement: ب، ت، ث، ن.
  • Show a partner the direction Arabic text is generally read.
  • Reflect: which two letter forms are easiest to confuse, and why?

Culture and context: Arabic is used in multiple countries and communities, in formal, colloquial, literary, artistic and everyday settings. Respect its regional diversity and varieties.

5. Week 01 knowledge quiz

Ten questions. Choose one answer per question. Open-book practice; a score of at least 8/10 indicates readiness to proceed, but does not replace the teacher's listening or writing check.

Teacher notes and evidence

Time: five flexible sessions of approximately 30–45 minutes. Evidence: a letter identification check, annotated handwriting sample, spoken sound distinction, and formative quiz. Accessibility: large letter cards, magnification, untimed attempts, keyboard-accessible quiz, and alternate writing accommodations. Do not grade speech solely by a browser synthesizer.

Misconceptions to monitor: assuming all Arabic letters connect on both sides; mixing up the number or location of dots; assuming a romanized letter name fully describes Arabic pronunciation; treating one spoken dialect as universal.

Assessment policy: quiz questions are formative and may be retried. The student's handwritten and oral demonstrations require separate observation. Nothing on this page automatically writes to a cross-device student record.
